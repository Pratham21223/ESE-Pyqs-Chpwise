## Unit 1: Understanding the Entrepreneurial Ecosystem

**Q1. Define entrepreneurship. What are its four pillars? (5 marks)**

Entrepreneurship is the process of designing, launching and running a new business by identifying an opportunity and organising resources to exploit it, while bearing the associated risks.

- Opportunity: spotting unmet needs.
- Innovation: creating new value (new product, method or market).
- Risk-taking: calculated, not reckless.
- Resources: organising people, money and technology.

Thinkers to quote: Schumpeter (entrepreneur = innovator driving "creative destruction"), Drucker (entrepreneurship is a discipline, not magic or genes; the entrepreneur searches for change, responds to it and exploits it), Cantillon (entrepreneur = risk-bearer, buys at certain prices and sells at uncertain ones).

**Q2. Differentiate between a startup and a traditional small business. (5 or 10 marks)**

| Basis | Startup | Small business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model | Innovative product, service or business model | Proven, existing model |
| Growth | Rapid, scalable, often tech-enabled | Steady, sustainable, linear |
| Market | Large or global from day one | Local or regional |
| Funding | Angels, VCs, bootstrapping toward scale | Own savings, family, bank loans |
| Risk | High risk, high uncertainty; searching for a repeatable model | Moderate, predictable |
| Goal | Exit: acquisition or IPO | Longevity: stable income for owners |

Also state: every startup involves entrepreneurship, but not every entrepreneurial act builds a startup.

**Q3. Differentiate entrepreneurship and a startup (second deck). (5 marks)**

Entrepreneurship: identifying, creating and pursuing opportunities in any business of any size; traits are innovation, risk-taking, creativity, persistence; success = sustainable profit and livelihood. Startup: newly established business designed for rapid growth with a scalable model under high uncertainty; traits are high growth potential, lean operations, disruption; seeks external funding; success = market disruption and high growth (exit or IPO).

**Q4. Explain the characteristics of successful entrepreneurs. (5 marks)**

1. Vision and creativity: sees opportunities where others see problems.
2. Calculated risk-taking: weighs risk against reward.
3. Resilience and persistence: treats failure as feedback.
4. Leadership and team-building: attracts talent, builds culture.
5. Market and customer focus: validates before scaling.
6. Decisiveness and adaptability: decides with incomplete data, pivots on evidence.

Second-deck version (five traits): innovation, risk-taking, creativity, adaptability, persistence.

**Q5. Key characteristics of a startup. (3 to 5 marks)**

High growth potential, innovative, lean operations (agile, minimal resources), disruption, entrepreneurial culture.

**Q6. Explain the startup lifecycle. What is the "Valley of Death"? (10 marks)**

1. Ideation: problem identified, idea formed, founding team assembles.
2. Validation: MVP built, customer discovery, product-market fit sought.
3. Early traction: first revenues, seed funding, core team hired.
4. Growth/Scaling: Series A and beyond, market expansion, processes formalised.
5. Maturity/Exit: sustained profits; IPO, acquisition or long-term operation.

Valley of Death = the period between initial funding and first sustainable revenue, when cash burn is high and income is low. Most failures happen here, which is exactly where incubators, accelerators and seed investors add the most value.

**Q7. Role of entrepreneurship in the economy. (10 marks)**

1. Employment generation: startups and MSMEs create jobs faster than large firms; each direct job creates indirect jobs.
2. GDP and wealth creation: new output, investment, taxable wealth.
3. Innovation and productivity: new products and models raise industry productivity (creative destruction).
4. Exports and global competitiveness: IT services, SaaS, D2C brands earn foreign exchange.
5. Balanced regional development: Tier-2/3 startups slow distress migration.
6. Social and inclusive impact: health, education, agriculture, fintech for the underserved.
7. Economic resilience and diversity: less dependence on one industry.
8. Policy implications: governments cut bureaucracy, protect IP, fund education and mentorship.

Add India figures: 1,50,000+ DPIIT-recognised startups (older deck) or 110,000+ (newer deck), 110+ unicorns (older deck) or 136 (newer deck), 16 lakh+ direct jobs, \~50% from Tier-2/3 cities, 3rd largest ecosystem. Your deck says to verify the latest numbers, so quote one set and label "as per Startup India/DPIIT".

Interplay: many founders validate locally and then scale globally, or partner with global firms while keeping local roots.

Infrastructure (space, labs, prototyping), mentorship, funding access (seed grants, demo days, investor introductions), networks (customers, suppliers, alumni, corporates), business services (legal, accounting, IP filing, compliance), structured learning (bootcamps, workshops). Why they matter: access to networks, structured mentorship, early capital and credibility, risk mitigation and faster iteration.

**Q19. Flipkart case study. (5 to 10 marks)**

Innovation: Cash on Delivery plus in-house logistics (Ekart), localised instead of copying the US model. Scaling: books to electronics and fashion, acquired Myntra, fought Amazon. **Q20. Zomato case study. (5 to 10 marks)**

Survived losses, layoffs and the 2020 pandemic with contactless delivery and cost discipline. Acquired Uber Eats India (2020) and Blinkit (2022). IPO July 2021, later profitable.

**Q21. Airbnb case study. (5 to 10 marks)**

Joined Y Combinator in 2009; mentors pushed them to meet users in New York and improve listings and photos. **Q22. Common patterns behind startup success. (5 marks)**

Start from a real problem; build trust systems (reviews, CoD, verified profiles); localise the solution; use the ecosystem (accelerators, investors, acquisitions); pivot on evidence (Zomato, Airbnb); survive crises with resilience and cash discipline.

**Q23. Product-based versus service-based startups, with a success and failure story each. (10 marks)**

| Basis | Product-based | Service-based |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upfront investment | High (R&D, design, manufacturing) | Low to moderate (people, process, platform) |
| Speed to market | Slower: build before selling | Faster: launch and learn |
| Scalability | High, especially software | Limited by people and quality unless platform-driven |
| Biggest risk | Building something nobody wants (Juicero) | Growth without sustainable delivery (Byju's) |
| Moat | IP, design, manufacturing, brand | Trust, network effects, retention, brand |

- Product success, boAt: founded 2016 by Aman Gupta and Sameer Mehta; design, price point and Gen-Z marketing; own distribution plus influencers; \~USD 1.5 billion peak valuation. Lesson: know your customer better than the "better" competitor, control your brand story.

- Service success, Urban Company: founded 2014 as UrbanClap; vetted professionals, training, background checks, standard pricing; two-sided marketplace; unicorn and IPO. Lesson: trust and consistent quality are the real product.
- Service failure, Byju's: grew by acquisitions (Aakash, WhiteHat Jr), spent on marketing over unit economics and governance; auditor resignation and delayed financials 2023; valuation USD 22 billion to \~0; insolvency proceedings 2024. Lesson: growth on marketing spend without retention and delivery runs out of runway.

One-line takeaway: products fail by over-building, services fail by over-scaling.

## Unit 2: Developing a Startup Mindset

**Q1. What is a startup mindset? How is it different from a managerial mindset? (5 to 10 marks)**

A startup mindset is a way of thinking oriented toward action, experimentation and rapid learning rather than certainty before moving. Assumptions are hypotheses to test with real customers; constraints are forcing functions for creativity. It asks "what is the smallest experiment that will teach us the most?" It is a trainable skill, not a fixed personality trait.

| Managerial/employee mindset | Entrepreneurial mindset |
| --- | --- |
| Optimises existing resources | Pursues opportunity beyond resources controlled |
| Avoids failure (career damage) | Treats failure as data; iterates fast and cheaply |
| Waits for complete information | Acts under uncertainty with calculated experiments |
| Follows defined roles | Wears multiple hats; owns outcomes |
| Rewards: salary, promotion, security | Rewards: equity, impact, autonomy |
| Causal logic: set goal, find means | Effectual logic: start with means you have, co-create goals |

**Q2. Explain the building blocks of the entrepreneurial mindset. (5 marks)**

Opportunity orientation (scanning problems and waste as businesses); comfort with ambiguity (running experiments instead of waiting); bias for action ("done and tested" beats "perfect and imagined"); growth mindset (Dweck); resourcefulness and bricolage (making do with what is at hand); long-term ownership (thinking in years).

**Q3. Fixed versus growth mindset. (5 marks)**

Fixed: talent is static, avoids challenges, failure = incompetence, feedback feels like attack, effort is pointless without natural gift. Growth (Carol Dweck): abilities develop through effort, seeks challenges, failure is data, feedback is fuel, effort is the path to mastery.

**Q4. Five habits that build the startup mindset. (5 marks)**

Bias toward action (ship a rough version this week); comfort with ambiguity (decide with about 70% of the information); customer obsession (talk to real users weekly); resourcefulness (solve with what is available before asking for budget); growth mindset. Quote: "If you are not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you've launched too late" (Reid Hoffman, LinkedIn).

**Q5. Core characteristics of entrepreneurs and the three beliefs behind the mindset. (5 marks)**

Calculated risk-taking, self-efficacy, passion and purpose, adaptability. Research links the mindset to self-efficacy, internal locus of control (outcomes come from my actions, not luck) and tolerance for ambiguity. It also applies inside companies (intrapreneurship).

**Q6. How do entrepreneurs manage risk? Explain the three filters. (5 marks)**

Risk is calculated, not reckless. Affordable loss: what is the most I can afford to lose, not the most I could gain? Reversibility: can the decision be undone or adjusted? Small bets: can I test the riskiest assumption cheaply before scaling? Case: Sara Blakely (Spanx) invested her USD 5,000 savings, wrote her own patent, was rejected by 20+ manufacturers, built a USD 1B+ company.

**Q7. How can the entrepreneurial mindset be built? (5 marks)**

Expose yourself to real problems (volunteer, shadow, customer-facing jobs); make small low-stakes decisions with real consequences; seek mentors who ask better questions; reflect after successes and failures; join a peer community. Exercise: list three problems you faced this month and why no one has solved them well.

**Q8. Case: Phool.co. (5 marks)**

Ankit Agarwal (2015) saw tonnes of pesticide-laden temple flowers dumped into the Ganga at Kanpur. Reframe: waste is a mispriced raw material. Product: charcoal-free incense sticks and cones from recycled temple flowers ("flowercycling"), D2C brand employing women from marginalised communities. Deep-tech leap: "Fleather", plant-based leather alternative, won PETA's Best Innovation in Vegan Fashion. Supported by IIT Kanpur SIIC. Shows opportunity orientation and resourcefulness.

**Q9. What is market analysis? List its components. (5 marks)**

A structured study of a market: size, trends, customers, competitors and regulation. It answers: is this market worth entering and can we win in it? Components: industry overview (size, growth, maturity, trends); customer analysis (who buys, why, what job the product is hired for); competitive landscape (direct, indirect, emerging); regulatory and economic factors. It feeds product decisions, pricing and fundraising narratives, and must be revisited continuously.

**Q10. Explain TAM, SAM and SOM with an example. (5 marks, very likely)**

- TAM (Total Addressable Market): everyone who could conceivably use the product category. Example: all two-wheeler buyers in India (\~1.7 to 1.8 crore vehicles a year).
- SAM (Serviceable Available Market): the slice your product and channels can serve. Example: electric scooter buyers in cities with charging infrastructure.
- SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market): what you can realistically capture in 2 to 3 years. Example: premium EV scooter buyers in 15 metro cities.

Investors ask for it to test whether you understand the customer. Bottom-up sizing (units x price x buyers) is more credible than top-down "1% of India". SOM drives the revenue plan and funding ask.

**Q11. Explain STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning). (10 marks)**

1. Segment: divide the market into groups with similar needs. Bases: demographic (age, income, occupation), geographic (metro, Tier-2, rural), psychographic (values, lifestyle, tech-affinity), behavioural (usage rate, price sensitivity, loyalty). Good segments are measurable, substantial, accessible and different from each other.
2. Target: choose segments you can win by evaluating size, growth, competition and fit. Use a beachhead strategy: dominate one narrow segment first. "Everyone is my customer" means no one is.
3. Position: own a clear place in the customer's mind. Positioning statement: For \[target\], \[brand\] is the \[category\] that \[key benefit\] because \[proof\]. Basis: price, performance, convenience or values; stay consistent across product, price and communication.

Over-segmentation wastes resources; under-segmentation gives generic messaging.

**Q12. Case: Ather Energy as beachhead and positioning. (5 to 10 marks)**

Tarun Mehta and Swapnil Jain (IIT Madras), incubated at IIT Madras Incubation Cell, began with battery R&D (2013). Insight: buyers saw e-scooters as cheap and slow; unserved segment was urban professionals wanting a premium smart scooter. Targeting: launched Ather 450 only in Bengaluru first. Positioning: "smart electric scooter" with touchscreen, OTA updates and Ather Grid fast charging, against petrol scooters, not cheap EVs. Backed by Hero MotoCorp; expanded city by city; went public 2025.

**Q13. Porter's Five Forces. (5 marks)**

Competitive rivalry; threat of new entrants; supplier power; buyer power; threat of substitutes. Used to judge industry attractiveness.

**Q14. Case: Nike segmentation. (3 marks)**

Nike segments by sport, performance level and lifestyle (Running, Basketball, Sportswear), each with tailored products, pricing and marketing.

**Q15. What is opportunity recognition? Where do opportunities come from? (10 marks)**

Opportunity recognition is identifying a gap between what the market offers and what customers need. An idea is only an opportunity if it is desirable, feasible and viable. It is a trainable skill.

Sources (with IIT incubated examples):

1. Personal pain points: Ather founders' frustration with poor lead-acid EVs.
2. Regulation and policy shifts: Chakr Innovation (IIT Delhi) and emission control; Chakr Shield captures diesel-generator soot and turns it into inks.
3. Industry inefficiencies: Detect Technologies (IIT Madras) and manual pipeline inspection; built AI asset monitoring.
4. New technology waves: Agnikul (IIT Madras) used metal 3D printing for affordable rocket engines; Agnilet is a single-piece printed engine.
5. Waste and by-products: Phool.co, temple flowers to incense and vegan leather.
6. Demographic and social trends: health awareness, urbanisation, EV adoption, digital payments.

**Q16. Drucker's four sources of innovation opportunity and the recognition process. (5 marks)**

Sources: unexpected occurrences; incongruities (gap between what should happen and what does); process needs (weak link in a process); industry and market shifts. Process: Perception (notice the problem) to Discovery (why it persists, who feels it) to Formation (shape a concept for the root cause) to Evaluation (test desirability, feasibility, viability).

**Q17. The opportunity screening filter. (5 marks)**

| Question | Tests | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the problem real and painful? | Customers already spend money or time on workarounds | Nice-to-have |
| Is the market big enough and growing? | TAM/SAM/SOM, trend | Shrinking or too small |
| Can WE win it? | Founder-market fit, unfair advantage | No special insight or capability |
| Is the timing right? | Enabling tech, policy, behaviour | Too early or too late |
| Can it make money? | Willingness to pay, unit economics | Users love it free but won't pay |

**Q18. Common pitfalls in opportunity recognition. (5 marks)**

Confusing personal preference with market need; falling in love with a solution before validating the problem; ignoring market timing; underestimating competitor reaction; over-weighting feedback from friends and family. Reflection test: would a stranger pay for this, and say yes a second time?

**Q19. Case: Chakr Innovation and Uber. (5 marks)**

Chakr: Delhi's air emergency; soot is nearly pure carbon so capturing it creates value twice (cleaner air plus sellable by-product); Chakr Shield is a retrofit on generator exhausts; founded by IIT Delhi graduates, nurtured via FITT; lesson: regulatory pressure plus waste-to-value insight. Uber: founders struggled to hail a cab in Paris in 2008; smartphones and GPS already existed; the opportunity was connecting existing technology to a badly served process.

**Q20. Define innovation. Distinguish invention and innovation. (5 marks)**

Innovation = converting new ideas into value (product, service or process) that meets market or societal needs. Invention creates something new; innovation makes it useful and adopted. An invention becomes an innovation only when brought to market and adopted by customers. Innovation needs creativity (novel ideas) and discipline (execution).

**Q21. Types of innovation with examples. (10 marks)**

- Product: new or improved goods (iPhone, EVs); Atomberg's BLDC fan cuts energy \~65%.
- Process: new methods of production or delivery (Amazon logistics, Just-in-Time); Agnikul prints a whole engine as one piece.
- Business model: new way of creating, delivering, capturing value (Netflix subscriptions; Ather sells scooter plus software plus charging).
- Service: better customer experience (Swiggy Instamart, Uber).
- Social: tackles societal challenges (Grameen Bank microfinance; SELCO solar for rural households).
- Incremental: small continuous improvements (each new fan or scooter version).
- Radical/breakthrough: new-to-the-world capability (E-Spin Nanotech nanofibre SWASA N95 respirators).
- Disruptive (Christensen): simpler or cheaper products that start at the low end or a new market and then displace incumbents (EVs versus petrol two-wheelers; budget airlines).

Sustaining innovation improves existing products for existing customers (premium smartphone generations); disruptive is higher risk but can redefine an industry.

**Q22. Techniques of idea generation. (10 marks)**

- Brainstorming: quantity first, no criticism, build on others ("yes, and"), wild ideas welcome, timebox 15 to 20 min, converge later.
- Brainwriting 6-3-5: 6 people write 3 ideas in 5 minutes and pass the sheet; 108 ideas in 30 minutes, no loud-voice bias.
- SCAMPER: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify/Magnify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse.
- Mind mapping: visual radiating associations.
- Observation/ethnography: watch users in context (Phool's Ganga moment).
- TRIZ: systematic innovation using 40 principles.
- Crowdsourcing: ideas from large groups online.
- NABC: Need, Approach, Benefits, Competition.
- Others: Six Thinking Hats, customer journey mapping, jobs-to-be-done, trend extrapolation, reverse brainstorming.

**Q23. Explain Design Thinking. (10 marks, very likely)**

Human-centred innovation in five non-linear stages: Empathise (interviews, observation, immersion); Define (frame user + need + insight as a problem statement); Ideate (diverge with brainstorming and SCAMPER, then converge); Prototype (cheap, rough, fast: paper, cardboard, 3D print, mock app); Test (put it in users' hands, learn, loop back). Teams loop constantly because testing reveals new insights. Example: Planys Technologies (IIT Madras) iterated underwater inspection robots through field trials with ports and dams.

**Q24. Frameworks for idea evaluation. (5 marks)**

Three lenses of innovation: Desirability (human), Feasibility (technical), Viability (business). Business Model Canvas: test value proposition, resources, channels, costs, revenue. Apple example: iterative, customer-centric design.

**Q25. What is feasibility analysis? Explain its types. (10 marks, very likely)**

A systematic evaluation of a business idea to see whether it is practical, profitable and sustainable before investing significant resources. Purpose: reduce risk, validate assumptions, optimise resources. Done before launch. A negative finding is a successful outcome because it saves the cost of a failed launch.

1. Market feasibility: target customers, market size (TAM/SAM/SOM), competition level, adoption rate and demand forecast.
2. Technical feasibility: can it be built with existing technology; infrastructure, equipment, skills; prototype readiness.
3. Financial feasibility: startup and operating costs, break-even, projected revenue and cash flow, ROI and payback period.
4. Operational feasibility: day-to-day running, supply chain, logistics, staffing, approvals.
5. Legal and environmental feasibility: laws, licences, permits, IP (patents, copyrights, trademarks), sustainability impact.

**Q26. Explain the TELOS framework with a case. (10 marks, very likely)**

- T, Technical: can we build it with available technology, skills and quality (prototype, manufacturability, component supply).
- E, Economic: cost structure, price points, unit economics, break-even volume, funding to reach revenue.
- L, Legal: certifications, licences, IP freedom-to-operate, sector rules (DGCA for drones, ARAI for EVs, IN-SPACe for launch vehicles).
- O, Operational: manufacturing capacity, supply chain, service network, hiring, quality systems.
- S, Scheduling: development timeline versus market window, regulatory timelines, competitor moves.

Case Agnikul Cosmos: Technical: proved Agnilet single-piece 3D-printed engine by test-firing before building the full Agnibaan vehicle. Economic: 3D printing cuts part count and labour for low-cost small-satellite launches. Legal: IN-SPACe authorisation under 2020 private-space reforms. Operational: India's first private launchpad at Sriharikota with ISRO support. Scheduling: milestone-based (engine test, SOrTeD suborbital flight 2024, orbital ambitions), each milestone unlocking funding.

**Q27. Break-even analysis. (5 marks)**

Break-even is where total revenue equals total cost, so fixed and variable costs are fully covered. Formula: Break-even units = Fixed costs / (Price minus Variable cost per unit). The slide chart shows break-even at 133 units. Common feasibility mistakes: checking only financial feasibility, optimistic CAC and churn, treating "someone said they'd buy" as willingness to pay, underestimating regulatory and supply constraints, treating feasibility as one-time.

**Q7. Financial projections and budgeting for a product startup. (10 marks)**

- Revenue model: units x price, by segment and channel, built bottom-up month by month.
- Cost structure: COGS/BOM per unit (variable) versus rent, salaries, tooling, certifications (fixed).
- Gross margin = (Price minus Variable cost) / Price.
- Break-even volume = Fixed costs / Contribution per unit.
- Cash-flow projection: when money actually arrives and leaves; inventory eats cash before sales pay back.
- Budgeting: 12 to 18 month operating budget, quarterly reviews, separate R&D, ops and marketing budgets.

**Q8. Define burn rate, runway, CAC, LTV, unit economics. (5 marks)**

- Burn rate: net cash consumed per month.
- Runway: months of cash left = Bank balance / Burn rate.
- CAC: cost to acquire one customer.
- LTV: lifetime value of one customer; aim LTV at least 3x CAC.
- Unit economics: profit or loss on ONE unit, all-in.

**Q9. Numerical template (practise this). (5 marks)**

Price Rs 4,000, variable cost Rs 2,500, fixed costs Rs 6,00,000 per year. Contribution per unit = 4,000 minus 2,500 = Rs 1,500. Break-even units = 6,00,000 / 1,500 = 400 units. Gross margin = 1,500 / 4,000 = 37.5%. Runway: bank Rs 12 lakh, burn Rs 3 lakh per month = 4 months.

**Q4. What are the criteria for patentability? (5 marks)**

- Novelty: new worldwide, not disclosed anywhere before filing, including by YOU (publish or demo first and you may destroy your own patent).
- Inventive step: non-obvious to a person skilled in the art; more than a routine combination.
- Industrial applicability: capable of being made or used in industry.

Excluded in India (Sec. 3): mere discoveries, mathematical methods, business methods as such, traditional knowledge, and more.

3. Complete specification and publication (18 months).
4. Request examination, receive First Examination Report (FER), respond to objections.
5. Grant, then renew annually; enforce for 20 years.

**Q8. Explain the 6-step patent search method. (10 marks, very likely)**

1. Define the invention: one sentence of problem + solution + key technical feature; list synonyms and jargon.
2. Keyword search: combine element keywords with AND/OR; iterate as you learn the field's vocabulary.
3. Classification search: find the CPC/IPC class of close hits (e.g., F02K for jet/rocket propulsion) and browse the class to catch documents keywords miss.
4. Citation mining: follow backward citations (what a close patent cites) and forward citations (who later cited it) to map the technology family tree.
5. Analyse claims: novelty is judged against CLAIMS, not titles or abstracts; compare your feature list to independent claims element by element.
6. Document and decide: record queries, hits, differences as evidence for your attorney; file, redesign or abandon.

**Q9. Describe the anatomy of a patent document. (5 to 10 marks)**

- Bibliographic data: numbers, dates (priority, filing, publication, grant), inventors, assignee; tells owner and expiry.
- Claims (the legal heart): independent claims define the broadest protection; dependent claims add fallback detail; infringement = practising every element of a claim.
- Abstract and drawings: quick orientation; screening only, never for legal conclusions.
- Classification codes (CPC/IPC): locate the document in the technology taxonomy; entry point for systematic searching.
- Description/specification: background, problem, detailed embodiments; must enable a skilled person to reproduce the invention.
- Legal status and family: granted, lapsed, opposed, countries filed; a lapsed patent is free to use, a pending one is a moving target.

**Q10. Explain IP protection strategies for startups. (10 marks, near-certain)**

1. Patent versus trade secret: patent what competitors could reverse-engineer from the product (mechanisms, structures); keep secret what they cannot see (process settings, formulations).
2. File early, file smart: provisional first for core inventions, complete within 12 months; never demo, publish or exhibit before the priority date.
3. Go global selectively: PCT route keeps 150+ countries open for about 30 months; nationalise only where you sell, manufacture or face competitors.
4. Use startup schemes: DPIIT-recognised startups get \~80% patent fee rebate, expedited examination, and SIPP (government-empanelled facilitators draft for free; you pay only statutory fees).
5. Hygiene and contracts: NDAs before disclosures; IP-assignment clauses in every employment and vendor contract; joint-development agreements define ownership up front.

**Q11. Six IP mistakes that kill startups. (5 to 10 marks)**

1. Self-disclosure: demo at a hackathon, publish a paper or post a video before filing destroys novelty worldwide.
2. Wrong geography: an India-only patent cannot stop a competitor making and selling in the US or EU.
3. Unclear founder/institute IP: tech built in a college lab without an IP agreement; sort ownership before incorporation.
4. Filing everything: patents cost lakhs over their life; protect crown jewels only.
5. No assignment clauses: an employee or freelancer who wrote your firmware may legally own it unless the contract assigns IP to the company.
6. Ignoring others' IP: no freedom-to-operate check leads to infringement notices; search before you scale.

**Q12. Ethics in technology and innovation. (10 marks)**

- Safety and testing honesty: never ship known-unsafe hardware; report test results truthfully to certifiers (vehicles, drones, medical devices).
- Data privacy: connected products collect personal data; minimise, secure, be transparent; DPDP Act compliance.
- AI and algorithmic fairness: vision and decision systems can encode bias; validate across users and publish limitations.
- Dual-use dilemmas: drones survey crops and battlefields alike; export controls and end-user checks are ethical duties.
- Environmental responsibility: batteries, e-waste, emissions; design for repair and recycling (ELV and battery-waste rules).
- Honest claims and IP respect: no inflated specs to investors or customers; respect others' patents and licences.

Conclusion: ethics is part of engineering, not an afterthought.

Access to capital; expertise and mentorship; networking and connections; credibility and validation (a stamp that attracts investors, talent and clients).

- Early stage: Series A of USD 5 million in 2012 from Tiger Global (as per your deck) for tech and marketing.
- Growth stage: 2014 round of about USD 210 million (Series D, led by SoftBank) to enter new cities and compete with Uber.
- Expansion: USD 1.1 billion in 2017 from Tencent, SoftBank and others to diversify into electric mobility, food delivery and financial services.
- Recent: Ola Electric raised USD 200 million in 2021 at about USD 5 billion valuation.

**Q10. What is crowdfunding? Explain the types and process. (10 marks)**

Crowdfunding is raising money by collecting small amounts of capital from a large number of people (Investopedia definition on your slide).

Types:

- Donation-based: contributions without expecting financial returns; social causes, charity, medical expenses. Example: Ketto.
- Reward-based: contributors get non-financial rewards (product sample, merchandise, experience). Popular with creative artists, filmmakers and product launches. Example: Wishberry.
- Equity crowdfunding: investors receive shares in the company.

Process: create a compelling plan and validate it; decide what rewards backers receive on reaching goals; post on a crowdfunding platform; promote the project; receive the money.

**Q11. Why do startups need financial modelling? (10 marks, very likely)**

1. Fundraising and securing investments: a robust framework presenting the startup's financial future to investors.
2. Strategic planning and decision-making: scenario analysis and "what if" testing of market size, pricing, costs and growth rates.
3. Cash flow management: anticipates inflows and outflows so deficits can be fixed early.
4. Performance monitoring: compare projected versus actual figures to spot underperformance and adjust.
5. Communication with stakeholders: a common language for investors, employees, partners and customers; builds trust and alignment.

**Q12. Key components of a startup financial model. (5 marks)**

- Revenue model: subscription, pay-per-use, freemium, licensing, direct sales. Example: a SaaS startup with Basic (USD 10/month) and Premium (USD 30/month) plans, modelling new customers each month and upgrades/downgrades.
- Expense model: fixed and variable costs and how they scale. Example: a D2C startup with manufacturing, shipping, marketing, customer support and salaries, with assumptions for ad-spend growth and CAC.

**Q13. How to craft an effective elevator pitch? (10 marks, very likely)**

Six rules, each with an example from your deck:

1. Keep it short and sweet: clear, concise, compelling, under a minute (EcoWraps reusable food wraps replacing plastic).
2. Focus on the problem: start with a specific pain point (60%+ of small businesses struggle to track expenses).
3. Explain why you are the best solution: what makes it effective and unique (SmartLedger automates expense tracking, no accounting expertise needed).
4. Describe your target market: small to medium retail businesses with annual revenue under Rs 50 lakh and no finance team.
5. Provide data points: market size and traction (50 million small businesses, Rs 5,000 crore market, 2,000 paying users in six months).
6. Ask for what you want: a specific call to action (raising Rs 1 crore to expand the dev team and reach 10,000 users).

Quick template: problem, solution, market, traction, ask.

**Q14. Where do incubators and accelerators fit in fundraising? (3 marks)**

Incubators: infrastructure, mentoring, seed grants and introductions with little or no equity. Accelerators: seed investment for \~5 to 10% equity, a demo day to pitch investors. Y Combinator gave Airbnb USD 20,000 for 6% in 2009.
