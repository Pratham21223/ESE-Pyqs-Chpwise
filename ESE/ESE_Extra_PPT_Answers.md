## X.1 Product-based vs service-based startups

**PPT source:** `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slides 23–29.

**Answer**

A **product startup** builds a tangible good or standalone software that customers buy, own or license. Value is delivered through the product itself. A **service startup** performs a task, delivers an experience, or connects supply and demand; value is delivered through an ongoing service.

| Dimension | Product-based | Service-based |
|---|---|---|
| **Upfront investment** | High — R&D, design, tooling, manufacturing | Low to moderate — people, process, platform |
| **Speed to market** | Slower; build before selling | Faster; launch and learn on the ground |
| **Scaling** | High, especially software (near-zero marginal cost) | Constrained by people and quality unless platform-driven |
| **Biggest risk** | Building something nobody wants (Juicero) | Chasing growth without sustainable delivery (BYJU’S) |
| **Moat** | IP, design, manufacturing efficiency, brand | Trust, network effects, retention, brand |
| **Examples** | boAt, Apple, Canva, Xiaomi | Urban Company, Zomato/Swiggy, Uber/Ola, Airbnb |

**Success/failure lessons**

- **boAt (product success):** focused on design, price point and Gen-Z marketing rather than the “best” spec; built its own distribution and influencer-led brand.
- **Juicero (product failure):** raised $120M+ for a $400 Wi-Fi juicer, but the proprietary packs could be squeezed by hand; over-engineered a problem nobody would pay a premium for.
- **Urban Company (service success):** solved trust in an unorganised market through training, background checks and standard pricing; scaled as a two-sided marketplace.
- **BYJU’S (service failure):** acquisition-led growth and marketing spend outran unit economics and governance; insolvency in 2024.

**Exam use:** if asked “product vs service”, draw the five-row table, then one success and one failure from each side.

**Reference:** `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slides 23–29.

## X.2 Barriers by founder background

**PPT source:** `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slides 31–36.

**Answer**

The deck frames entrepreneurship as **not a level playing field**: the barriers differ by starting point, but both are real.

**Business-class founder**

| Barrier | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Comfort trap** | A stable inheritance, family business or career removes the urgency to take risk |
| **Legacy pressure** | Family expects continuity, not a risky new bet |
| **“Nepotism” perception** | Success is attributed to family money/connections, undermining credibility |
| **Fear of public failure** | Family reputation is on the line; failure is more visible |
| **Risk aversion by default** | Wealth is built to be preserved, not gambled |
| **Weak hunger/grit** | Discipline has to be self-generated, not forced by financial need |
| **Insulated market understanding** | Distance from cost-conscious, mass-market realities |
| **Proving merit** | Must build a team/product/traction that stand on their own |

**Working-class founder**

| Barrier | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Access to capital** | No savings cushion or collateral to bootstrap an MVP |
| **Thin networks** | Fewer connections to angels, mentors and operators |
| **No safety net** | Failure threatens basic family stability |
| **Unequal education/exposure** | Less early exposure to the “hidden curriculum” of startups |
| **Opportunity cost of time** | Time building is time not earning a stable income |
| **Credibility gap** | Investors may trust polish/pedigree over raw ability |
| **Bureaucracy alone** | Registration, compliance and contracts without help |
| **Necessity as advantage** | Closeness to real problems and resourcefulness |

**Key insight:** neither background guarantees success. Business-class founders must counter complacency and prove merit; working-class founders must find capital, mentors and business literacy. The deck’s examples — Nykaa’s Falguni Nayar, Ola’s Bhavish Aggarwal, Zomato’s Deepinder Goyal — show both paths breaking their own barrier.

**Reference:** `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slides 31–36.

## X.3 Who makes up an entrepreneurial ecosystem

**PPT source:** `Unit1_Entrepreneurial_Ecosystem.pptx`, slide 14; `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slide 3.

**Answer**

An ecosystem is the interconnected set of stakeholders, resources and conditions that determine whether entrepreneurs thrive.

| Component | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Entrepreneurs and talent** | Founders, employees, mentors, serial entrepreneurs |
| **Capital providers** | Angels, VCs, banks, government funds, crowdfunding |
| **Academia and research** | Universities, R&D labs, E-Cells, student incubators |
| **Government and policy** | Regulation, taxation, IP protection, procurement, startup policy |
| **Support organisations** | Incubators, accelerators, co-working spaces, NASSCOM, TiE |
| **Markets and corporates** | Early customers, corporate venture arms, acquirers |
| **Culture and society** | Attitudes to risk, failure and entrepreneurship |
| **Infrastructure and technology** | Connectivity, digital public infrastructure, labs |
| **Legal and regulatory environment** | Company law, IP rights, consumer protection, data rules |
| **Market dynamics** | Size, growth and competitiveness of the market |

**Why it matters:** a founder does not build alone. Capital, talent, policy, customers and culture all shape survival. India’s ecosystem — Startup India, DPIIT, SIDBI/FFS, AIM, incubators and a large domestic market — is the concrete example.

**Reference:** `Unit1_Entrepreneurial_Ecosystem.pptx`, slide 14; `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slide 3.

## X.4 Global case studies — innovation and resilience

**PPT source:** `Entrepreneurship_Startup_Ecosystem (1).pptx`, slides 39–41.

**Answer**

| Company | Key innovation | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Airbnb** (2008) | Peer-to-peer home rentals; trust through verified profiles, two-way reviews, secure payments and professional photography | Trust is the product in a marketplace |

## X.5 Porter’s Five Forces

**PPT source:** `Developing a Startup Mindset.pptx`, slide 18.

**Answer**

Porter’s Five Forces assesses **industry attractiveness** — how much profit is structurally available before a firm’s own strategy.

| Force | Question | Startup implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Competitive rivalry** | How intense is competition among existing players? | High rivalry compresses price and margin; differentiate or niche down |
| **Threat of new entrants** | How easily can new competitors enter? | Low barriers mean your idea will be copied; build brand/IP/network effects |
| **Supplier power** | How much leverage do suppliers have? | Single-source dependencies (e.g., battery cells) squeeze cost; dual-source |
| **Buyer power** | How much can customers demand? | Concentrated buyers push prices down; diversify the customer base |
| **Threat of substitutes** | What alternatives solve the same need? | Petrol scooters, public transport and cheap EVs are substitutes for Ather |

**How a startup uses it:** map the five forces for the chosen segment; if the industry is structurally unattractive, find a sub-segment where the forces are weaker or build a position that changes them (IP, distribution, switching costs). Ather’s response to intense rivalry and substitutes was technology, design and a charging network — not price.

**Exam use:** draw the five-force diagram, score each force high/medium/low, then state whether to enter and how to defend.

**Reference:** `Developing a Startup Mindset.pptx`, slide 18.

## X.6 Idea-evaluation frameworks — NABC, three lenses, BMC

**PPT source:** `Module 2 Part 2.pptx`, slide 6; `Unit3 Business Model Development(1).pptx`, slide 5.

**Answer**

Three complementary filters, from quick to detailed:

1. **NABC** — **N**eed (is the customer problem real?), **A**pproach (how does the solution solve it?), **B**enefits (what measurable value?), **C**ompetition (why is this better than alternatives?). Best for a 60-second screen.
2. **Three lenses of innovation** — **Desirability** (do people want it?), **Feasibility** (can we build it?), **Viability** (can it make money?). All three must hold; a “yes” on two is not enough.
3. **Business Model Canvas** — nine blocks (partners, activities, resources, value proposition, relationships, channels, segments, costs, revenue). Best for testing whether the whole model hangs together.

**Sequence:** NABC to kill weak ideas → three lenses to test the survivors → BMC to design and stress-test the business model → Lean Startup experiments to validate the riskiest block.

**Example:** A campus tiffin idea. NABC: need (hostellers want affordable home food), approach (subscription + two delivery slots), benefit (price/health/time), competition (canteen, Swiggy). Three lenses: desirable (survey), feasible (one kitchen), viable (₹30 contribution, 1,000 meals BEP). BMC: segments, channels, costs, revenue.

**Reference:** `Module 2 Part 2.pptx`, slide 6; `Unit3 Business Model Development(1).pptx`, slide 5.

## X.7 Creative problem-solving and extra idea techniques

**PPT source:** `Developing a Startup Mindset.pptx`, slides 31, 51–54; `Module 2 Part 2.pptx`, slide 5.

**Answer**

**Creative problem-solving framework (4 steps)**

1. **Clarify** — define the real problem, not the first symptom; ask “why” repeatedly.
2. **Ideate** — generate many solutions without judging.
3. **Develop** — select and strengthen the promising ones.
4. **Implement** — build a small test, gather feedback, refine.

**Additional techniques beyond brainstorming/SCAMPER**

| Technique | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Brainwriting 6-3-5** | 6 people write 3 ideas in 5 minutes, pass the sheet; 108 ideas in 30 minutes | Quiet teams; avoiding loud-voice bias |
| **Six Thinking Hats** | Examine an idea from six deliberate perspectives (facts, feelings, risks, benefits, creativity, process) | Avoiding one-sided decisions |
| **Customer journey mapping** | Map every touchpoint and friction | Service design |
| **Jobs-to-be-Done interviews** | Ask what “job” the customer hires the product to do | Understanding true demand |
| **TRIZ** | 40 inventive principles for resolving technical contradictions | Engineering/design problems |
| **Crowdsourcing** | Collect ideas from a large group/platform | Open innovation |
| **Reverse brainstorming** | “How can we make customers hate the product?” then reverse the answers | Unblocking a stuck team |

**Rule:** separate **divergence** (generate) from **convergence** (select). Mixing them shuts down creativity.

**Reference:** `Developing a Startup Mindset.pptx`, slides 31, 51–54; `Module 2 Part 2.pptx`, slide 5.

## X.8 Business model patterns

**PPT source:** `Unit3 Business Model Development(1).pptx`, slide 4.

**Answer**

| Pattern | How value is created and captured | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **D2C** | Sell direct online; own brand, data and margin | Atomberg, Phool |
| **Hardware + software + service** | Product plus subscription/connected features | Ather (scooter + software + charging) |
| **B2B / industrial** | Sell to businesses on specs and ROI | Chakr Shield, Detect, Planys |
| **Razor and blades** | Cheap base product, recurring consumable margin | Printers/ink; water-purifier filters |
| **Platform / marketplace** | Match two sides, take a commission; network effects | Flipkart, Zomato |

## X.9 Patent anatomy and six IP mistakes

**PPT source:** `Unit4 Innovation and IP(1).pptx`, slides 13, 16.

**Answer**

**Anatomy of a patent document**

| Part | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Bibliographic data** | Numbers, priority/filing/publication/grant dates, inventors, assignee |
| **Abstract and drawings** | Quick technical orientation; useful for screening, not legal conclusions |
| **Description/specification** | Background, problem, embodiments; must enable a skilled person to reproduce |
| **Claims** | The legal heart: independent claims define the broadest scope; dependent claims add fallback |
| **Classification codes** | CPC/IPC location in the technology taxonomy; entry point for searching |
| **Legal status and family** | Granted/lapsed/opposed; which countries; a lapsed patent is free to use |

**Six IP mistakes that kill startups**

1. **Self-disclosure** — demoing/publishing before filing destroys novelty.
2. **Unclear founder/institute IP** — tech built in a college lab without an agreement.
3. **No assignment clauses** — an employee/freelancer may own the code they wrote.
4. **Wrong geography** — an India-only patent cannot stop US/EU manufacturing.
5. **Filing everything** — patents cost lakhs; protect the crown jewels only.
6. **Ignoring others’ IP** — no freedom-to-operate search before launch.

**Rule:** read **claims**, not abstracts. Infringement means practising every element of a claim.

**Reference:** `Unit4 Innovation and IP(1).pptx`, slides 13, 16.

## X.10 Venture capital — types and how to secure it

**PPT source:** `Chapter 05.pptx`, slides 14–17.

**Answer**

**Types of VC by stage**

| Stage | What the startup is doing | What the money funds |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-seed** | Turning an idea into a concrete plan; may join an accelerator | Team, validation, first prototype |
| **Seed** | Launching the first product; no revenue yet | Product launch, early customers |
| **Early-stage (Series A/B)** | Product exists; needs production and sales scale-up | Hiring, manufacturing, market expansion |

**How to secure VC funding**

1. **Submit a business plan** — model, product, team, operating history.
2. **Due diligence** — the VC investigates the market, financials, team and IP.
3. **Investment pledge** — capital in exchange for equity, usually in rounds, with active monitoring.
4. **Exit** — typically 4–6 years after investment, via merger, acquisition or IPO.

**What VCs bring:** capital, expertise/mentorship, networks, credibility. What they expect: a large market, traction, a defensible edge and a credible path to a fund-level return (the deck cites up to ~10x on winners).

**Example — Ola:** Series A ($5M, Tiger Global, 2012) → Series D (~$210M, SoftBank, 2014) → $1.1B (2017) → Ola Electric ($200M at ~$5B, 2021). Each round matched a stage.

**Reference:** `Chapter 05.pptx`, slides 14–17.

## X.11 Crowdfunding — the process

**PPT source:** `Chapter 05.pptx`, slides 20–23.

**Answer**

**Crowdfunding process**

```
Plan the campaign → Validate the idea/prototype → Announce rewards
→ Promote the campaign → Post on the platform → Receive funds
```

**Models**

| Model | Backer receives | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Donation** | No financial return; social impact | Ketto |
| **Reward** | Product, merchandise or experience | Wishberry |
| **Equity** | Shares (tightly regulated in India; check SEBI rules) | — |
| **Peer-to-peer lending** | Interest/repayment | Regulated platforms |

**What makes a campaign work:** a clear story and video, a realistic funding goal, tiered rewards, early backers, regular updates and social sharing. **Risks:** fulfilment delays, platform fees, and reputational damage if rewards are not delivered.

**Reference:** `Chapter 05.pptx`, slides 20–23.

## X.12 Entrepreneurship and the SDGs

**PPT source:** `Unit1_Entrepreneurial_Ecosystem.pptx`, slides 36–37.

**Answer**

The UN’s **17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** aim to end poverty, protect the planet and ensure prosperity by 2030. Startups contribute through market-based solutions:

| SDG | Entrepreneurial contribution | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — No Poverty** | Jobs, income growth, community upliftment | MSMEs and livelihood startups |
| **2 — Zero Hunger** | Agri-tech, precision farming, supply-chain optimisation | Farm-to-fork ventures |
| **3 — Good Health & Well-being** | Affordable healthcare, telemedicine, diagnostics | Niramai, health-tech |
| **4 — Quality Education** | E-learning, upskilling, vocational training | Ed-tech platforms |

**Why it matters for ESE:** it connects entrepreneurship to **social impact** and impact investing. A startup can pursue profit and SDG outcomes together — Phool (waste management), Chakr (air pollution), SELCO (solar energy) are examples.

**Reference:** `Unit1_Entrepreneurial_Ecosystem.pptx`, slides 36–37.
