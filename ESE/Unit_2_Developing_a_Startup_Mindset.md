## 2.1 Entrepreneurial mindset — characteristics **[5]**

**Asked in:** ESE-F24 Q2(a); ESE-R25 Q2(a).

**Questions (verbatim):**
> Explain any three characteristics essential for creating an entrepreneurial mindset with real life examples.
>
> Mr ‘X’ has a very good entrepreneurial mindset. Which characteristics do you think would he be possessing. List and explain them in detail.

**Answer**

**Definition.** The entrepreneurial mindset is a set of attitudes and skills that helps a person **identify opportunities, mobilise resources and create value under uncertainty**. It is not a fixed personality trait; it can be built through education, mentorship and repeated real decisions. It rests on three reinforcing beliefs: **self-efficacy**, an **internal locus of control** and **tolerance for ambiguity**.

**Managerial vs entrepreneurial mindset**

| Dimension | Managerial / employee mindset | Entrepreneurial mindset |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Optimise existing resources and processes | Pursue opportunity beyond current resources |
| **Failure** | Failure damages the career; avoid it | Failure is data; iterate fast and cheaply |
| **Information** | Wait for complete information | Act under uncertainty with small experiments |
| **Role** | Defined job description | Multiple hats; ownership of outcomes |
| **Reward** | Salary, promotion, security | Equity, impact, autonomy, delayed gratification |
| **Logic** | Causal: set a goal, find the means | Effectual: start with means, co-create the goal |

**Key characteristics (explain any three in the exam)**

1. **Opportunity orientation.** The founder scans problems, inefficiencies and waste as potential businesses. *Example:* Ankit Agarwal saw tonnes of temple flowers being dumped into the Ganga at Kanpur and built **Phool.co** — incense from recycled flowers, later “Fleather” vegan leather (IIT Kanpur SIIC).
2. **Comfort with ambiguity and calculated risk.** The founder acts with incomplete data and limits the downside through **affordable loss, reversibility and small bets**. *Example:* Airbnb’s founders rented air mattresses during a conference instead of waiting for a perfect plan; Sara Blakely started SPANX with $5,000 and cold-called buyers after manufacturers rejected her.
3. **Bias for action.** Build a rough version, test it with real users, learn, repeat — “done and tested” beats “perfect and imagined”. *Example:* Airbnb tested a rough idea immediately and iterated for years; Reid Hoffman’s line — if you are not embarrassed by the first version, you launched too late.
4. **Growth mindset (Carol Dweck).** Ability grows with effort; feedback and failure are inputs, not verdicts.
5. **Resourcefulness and bricolage.** Make do with what is at hand — campus labs, hostel networks, personal savings — instead of waiting for ideal resources.
6. **Long-term ownership and persistence.** Think in years, accept delayed rewards and take responsibility for outcomes. *Example:* SPANX’s years of rejection before a billion-dollar valuation.
7. **Self-efficacy and internal locus of control.** Belief that outcomes come from one’s own actions, built from small proven wins.

**Conclusion.** The mindset is learnable. For “Mr X”, the strongest answer picks three traits, defines each, gives one real founder example and links it to behaviour — not just adjectives.

## 2.2 Market analysis and segmentation **[5]**

**Asked in:** ESE-F24 Q2(b); ESE-R25 Q2(b).

**Questions (verbatim):**
> What are the main types of Market Segmentation? Explain any three.
>
> Explain in detail the role of Market Analysis and Segmentation in detail.

**Answer**

**Market analysis** is a structured study of a market — its size, growth, customers, competitors and regulation. It answers: *is this market worth entering, and can we win in it?* Its core components are the **industry overview, customer analysis, competitive landscape, and regulatory/economic factors**.

**Market sizing — TAM, SAM, SOM**

| Level | Meaning | Ather example |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | Total addressable market — everyone who could use the category | All two-wheeler buyers in India |
| **SAM** | Serviceable available market — the slice your product and channels can serve | Electric two-wheelers in cities with charging infrastructure |
| **SOM** | Serviceable obtainable market — what you can realistically capture in 2–3 years | Premium EV scooter buyers in 15 metro cities |

Bottom-up sizing (units × price × buyers) is more credible than “1% of India”.

**Segmentation** divides a broad market into groups of buyers with shared needs, characteristics or behaviour. A good segment is **measurable, substantial, accessible and differentiable**. Segmentation is the foundation of **STP — Segment, Target, Position**.

**Main types of segmentation**

| Base | Divides buyers by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Demographic** | Age, income, education, occupation, family size | Budget airline targeting price-sensitive travellers under 35 |
| **Geographic** | Country, region, city, climate, urban/rural | Ather launching first in Bengaluru |
| **Psychographic** | Lifestyle, values, personality, social class | Fitness app for “goal-driven achievers” |
| **Behavioural** | Purchase occasion, usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought | Nike’s perform / train / lifestyle lines |

**Explain any three (exam-ready)**

- **Demographic:** easiest to measure and target through media; income and age predict ability to pay. *Example:* premium scooter priced for urban professionals.
- **Geographic:** city-level density, climate and infrastructure change demand. *Example:* Ather’s Bengaluru beachhead before national expansion.
- **Psychographic:** values and lifestyle explain why two customers with the same income buy differently. *Example:* energy-conscious households buying Atomberg fans on a “65% less electricity” promise.
- **Behavioural:** usage and loyalty are often the best predictor of repeat purchase; enables loyalty tiers and occasion-based offers.

**Role and benefits of segmentation**

1. Stronger, specific marketing messages instead of generic advertising.
2. Efficient targeting and lower customer-acquisition cost.
3. Better product and pricing decisions.
4. Attracts the *right* customers and improves retention/loyalty.
5. Differentiates the brand from competitors.
6. Uncovers niche and underserved segments.
7. Focuses scarce startup resources on a **beachhead** instead of “everyone”.

**STP and positioning.** After segmenting, choose the segment you can win (size, growth, competition, founder–market fit), then own a place in the customer’s mind. Positioning statement: *For [target], [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit] because [proof].*

**Example — Ather Energy.** It segmented the two-wheeler market by technology and lifestyle, targeted urban professionals who wanted a premium smart scooter, and positioned the Ather 450 against petrol scooters — not cheap EVs. It launched in one dense, tech-friendly city first, then expanded as charging density grew.

**Conclusion.** Market analysis sizes the prize; segmentation and STP decide which slice to attack first. A startup that targets everyone usually reaches no one.

## 2.3 Feasibility analysis and idea evaluation **[5–8]**

**Asked in:** ESE-F24 Q2(c); ESE-R25 Q2(c); ESE-F25 Q3B(i).

**Questions (verbatim):**
> Enlist the key factors essential while doing the Feasibility Analysis for a business idea and explain any three.
>
> What is Feasibility Analysis of Business Ideas? Explain it with the help of an example.
>
> Explain the key steps involved in evaluating whether a new idea can become a successful business. Give one simple example.

**Answer**

**Definition.** Feasibility analysis is the **systematic evaluation of a business idea** to decide whether it is practical, profitable and sustainable **before** committing significant money, time or reputation. It is a cheap filter that prevents an expensive failure.

**Key factors / dimensions**

| Dimension | Question it answers | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Is there real demand? | Target customers, TAM/SAM/SOM, competition, adoption rate |
| **Technical** | Can we build it? | Technology readiness, skills, infrastructure, prototype, manufacturability |
| **Financial** | Do the numbers work? | Startup and operating costs, revenue, margin, break-even, ROI, payback, cash flow |
| **Operational** | Can we run it daily? | Supply chain, logistics, staffing, service network, quality systems |
| **Legal / regulatory** | Are we allowed? | Licences, certifications, IP freedom to operate, sector rules |
| **Environmental / social** | Is it responsible? | Sustainability, waste, safety, community impact |
| **Scheduling** | Can we do it in time? | Development timeline vs market window, regulatory timelines |

**TELOS framework** — **T**echnical, **E**conomic, **L**egal, **O**perational, **S**cheduling. This is the exam-friendly way to remember the dimensions.

**Steps in evaluating an idea**

1. **Define the concept** in one testable sentence: customer + problem + solution.
2. **Test each dimension** (market, technical, financial, operational, legal, scheduling).
3. **Identify deal-breakers** — a single failed dimension can stop the idea.
4. **Decide: go, pivot or stop.**
5. **Revisit** as new information arrives; feasibility is not a one-time report.

**Screening filter (five questions)** — is the problem real and painful? Is the market big and growing? Can *we* win it (founder–market fit)? Is the timing right? Can it make money?

**Example — Agnikul Cosmos (IIT Madras)**

- **Technical:** proved the core risk first — **Agnilet**, a single-piece 3D-printed rocket engine, was test-fired before building a full rocket.
- **Economic:** 3D printing collapses part count and labour, enabling low-cost, on-demand small-satellite launches.
- **Legal:** operated within India’s private-space framework (IN-SPACe authorisation).
- **Operational:** built a private launchpad at Sriharikota with ISRO support.
- **Scheduling:** milestone-based — engine test → suborbital flight (2024) → orbital ambition, each unlocking the next funding round.

**Simple example (for the 8-mark variant).** A campus tiffin service: *market* — 2,000 hostellers, 300 willing to pay; *technical* — one kitchen, two delivery bikes; *financial* — ₹80 per meal, ₹50 variable cost, ₹30,000 fixed cost ⇒ break-even 1,000 meals/month; *operational* — two delivery slots; *legal* — FSSAI licence; *scheduling* — launch in one hostel before scaling. If the numbers fail, stop before buying equipment.

**Conclusion.** Feasibility analysis converts an attractive idea into a go/no-go decision. The best answer shows the framework, applies it to one example, and states the decision.

## 2.4 Innovation — meaning, role and types **[5–8]**

**Asked in:** ESE-F24 Q2(d); ESE-R25 Q2(d); ESE-F25 Q3B(ii).

**Questions (verbatim):**
> What is Innovation? How does it help a startup? Explain with an example.
>
> Compare two business models, one which has used innovation and other which has not used. Hence highlight the significance of Innovation in Entrepreneurship.
>
> Explain how innovation supports entrepreneurship. Give one example of a business that grew because of innovation.

**Answer**

**Definition.** Innovation is the process of **converting a new idea into value** — a product, service, process or business model that customers adopt. **Invention** creates something new; **innovation** makes it useful and adopted. Creativity generates the idea; innovation executes and scales it.

**Types**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | New or improved good/service | Smartphone; Atomberg BLDC fan |
| **Process** | New way of producing or delivering | Amazon logistics; Agnikul’s 3D-printed engine |
| **Business model** | New way of creating and capturing value | Netflix subscription; Ather scooter + software + charging |
| **Service** | Better customer experience | Swiggy Instamart; Uber |
| **Social** | Solving a societal problem | Grameen microfinance; SELCO solar |
| **Incremental / radical / disruptive** | Small improvement / new-to-world / simpler-cheaper entrant that moves upmarket | Fan versions / nanofibre membrane / EVs vs petrol scooters |

**How innovation helps a startup**

1. **Differentiation** in a crowded market.
2. **Value creation** — a better solution to a real problem.
3. **Scalability** — a unique model can grow without proportional cost.
4. **Sustainability** — adaptation as technology and customer needs change.
5. **Disruption** — challenges incumbents and creates new categories.
6. **Barriers to imitation** — patents, brand, network effects, proprietary know-how.
7. **Pricing power and investor interest** — innovation supports premium pricing and a stronger funding story.

**Compare two business models**

| Dimension | Innovative model — Netflix | Non-innovative model — traditional DVD rental |
|---|---|---|
| **Offer** | Flat monthly subscription; no late fees | Per-title rental with late fees |
| **Delivery** | DVD-by-mail, then streaming | Physical store visits |
| **Scaling** | Near-zero marginal cost online; global | Each new market needs stores and inventory |
| **Customer relationship** | Recommendation engine, original content | Transactional, store-dependent |
| **Outcome** | 260M+ subscribers; redefined entertainment | Lost relevance as streaming grew |

Netflix did not invent video rental or streaming technology. Its innovation was the **business model** — removing friction and penalties, then pivoting from DVDs to streaming as bandwidth improved.

**Example — Tesla.** Tesla combined **product** innovation (long-range batteries), **process** innovation (over-the-air software updates) and **business-model** innovation (direct-to-consumer sales, bypassing dealers). Layered innovation made it hard for legacy automakers to copy one feature and catch up.

**Schumpeter’s view.** Entrepreneurship is “creative destruction”: new combinations of resources displace outdated products, firms and industries. Without innovation, a new venture is merely a copy competing on price with less capital.

**Conclusion.** Innovation is the engine that turns an opportunity into a defensible business. It supports entrepreneurship through differentiation, scalability and disruption — but only when the idea is adopted by paying customers.

## 2.5 Creativity — significance for a startup **[5]**

**Asked in:** ESE-F24 Q2(e); ESE-R25 Q2(e).

**Questions (verbatim):**
> Enlist the ways in which Creativity helps in fostering a business. Explain with an example.
>
> Explain the significance of Creativity in a startup. Explain with an example.

**Answer**

**Definition.** Creativity is the ability to generate ideas that are **both novel and useful** — novelty × usefulness. Novelty alone (a strange idea) or usefulness alone (a safe copy) is not creativity. It is a learnable skill shaped by habits, environment and practice.

**How creativity helps a business**

1. **Problem solving:** finds unconventional solutions to customer and operational problems.
2. **Differentiation:** unique products, messages and experiences stand out in crowded markets.
3. **Adaptability:** helps the startup pivot when technology, regulation or demand shifts.
4. **Innovation pipeline:** feeds new products, processes and business models.
5. **Cost-effective resource use:** constraints become design inputs (“design a cooler under ₹500”).
6. **Team motivation and engagement:** a creative culture attracts and retains better people.
7. **Long-term sustainability:** continuous creativity keeps the venture relevant.
8. **Critical thinking:** separates the real problem from the first symptom.

**Blockers vs boosters**

| What kills creativity | What grows it |
|---|---|
| Premature judgement while generating | Separate divergence from convergence |
| Fear of looking foolish | Psychological safety — wild ideas earn thanks |
| Functional fixedness | Cross-pollination across disciplines |
| Expert bias (“not how our industry works”) | Exposure — factory visits, user shadowing |
| Homogeneous teams | Diverse teams across branch and experience |
| No slack; back-to-back schedules | Protected time and idea quotas |

**Techniques to foster it:** brainstorming (quantity first, no criticism), brainwriting 6-3-5, **SCAMPER** (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse), mind mapping, observation/ethnography, design thinking, constraint framing, incubation time.

**Example — 3M Post-it Note.** Spencer Silver developed a low-tack adhesive that was considered a failure because it did not stick permanently. Art Fry later used it to keep bookmarks in a hymnal without damaging pages. 3M’s **15% time** for independent exploration allowed the “failed” adhesive to be reconsidered and launched as the Post-it Note — a product sold in 150+ countries.

**Indian example — Phool.co.** Temple flower waste became incense and later “Fleather”; the creative reframe was “waste = mispriced raw material”, not “garbage disposal”.

**Conclusion.** Creativity is the raw material of innovation. In a startup it improves problem solving, differentiation, adaptability and team motivation; the founder’s job is to build an environment where it can happen.

## 2.6 Syllabus bridge — opportunity recognition and idea generation **[not asked]**

**Asked in:** — *(printed in the syllabus; not directly asked in ESE-F24, ESE-R25 or ESE-F25).*

**Why it is here:** the syllabus lists **Opportunity Recognition** and **Innovation and Idea Generation** as separate topics; the papers test them only through feasibility and innovation questions. This section covers the missing definitions so the unit is complete.

**Opportunity recognition** is the cognitive process of spotting a gap between what the market offers and what customers need. An idea becomes an opportunity only if it passes the **three-part test: desirable, feasible and viable**.

**Where opportunities come from**

- Personal pain points — Ather’s founders’ frustration with poor EVs.
- Industry inefficiencies — Detect Technologies’ pipeline inspection.
- Waste and by-products — Phool’s temple flowers; Chakr’s diesel soot.
- Regulation and policy shifts — emission-control pressure that created Chakr Shield.
- New technology waves — Agnikul’s metal 3D printing.
- Demographic and social trends — health awareness, urbanisation, digital payments.

**Drucker’s sources:** unexpected occurrences, incongruities, process needs, industry/market shifts (the deck lists these four).

**Process:** perception → discovery → formation → evaluation.

**Common pitfalls:** confusing personal preference with market need; falling in love with the solution; ignoring timing; underestimating incumbents; relying on friends and family instead of unbiased strangers.

**Idea-generation toolkit:** brainstorming, brainwriting 6-3-5, SCAMPER, mind mapping, observation/ethnography, design thinking (empathise → define → ideate → prototype → test), TRIZ (40 principles), crowdsourcing.

**Evaluation frameworks:** NABC (Need, Approach, Benefits, Competition); three lenses (desirability, feasibility, viability); Business Model Canvas.
